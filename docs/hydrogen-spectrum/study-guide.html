The Atomic Spectrum of Hydrogen

Run an electric current through a tube of hydrogen gas and it glows. Spread that glow with a prism and you do not get a rainbow. You get a handful of sharp, separate lines. Explaining why those lines sit exactly where they do took thirty years and started quantum mechanics.

Part 1

Why a few coloured lines matter

Hydrogen's spectrum looks like a curiosity. It is one of the most consequential measurements in the history of science, and it reaches into astronomy, lighting, and the rest of this course.

It proved that energy in atoms comes in steps

A hot solid glows with every colour at once. Hydrogen gas glows with only a few. In 1885 a Swiss schoolteacher, Johann Balmer, found a simple formula that fit the four visible lines to four decimal places, but nobody knew why it worked. In 1913 Niels Bohr showed that the formula follows if the electron can only hold certain energies. Each line is a photon whose energy is the gap between two of those allowed levels. Sharp lines mean sharp levels: quantization was no longer a guess.

Bohr's equation for the levels, the one you use in the lab, won him the 1922 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Every element has a fingerprint

Because each element has its own set of levels, each has its own set of lines. Spectroscopy identifies elements from their light alone. Helium was discovered in 1868 as an unexplained yellow line in sunlight, 27 years before anyone found it on Earth. The flame tests in your Qualitative Analysis lab work the same way: the colour of the flame is the element's strongest visible line.

It is how astronomers read the universe

Light is the only thing that reaches us from a star. Hydrogen's lines appear in nearly every one, so they are the reference marks of astronomy. The red glow of nebulae in telescope images is the 656 nm hydrogen line. When Edwin Hubble found in 1929 that the lines of distant galaxies were shifted toward the red, the shift told him the universe is expanding.

Fraunhofer's dark lines C and F in the solar spectrum, catalogued in 1814, are hydrogen's 656 and 486 nm lines being absorbed by the Sun's own atmosphere.

It is in the room with you

A neon sign is a discharge tube; its orange-red is neon's emission lines. Fluorescent lamps run on mercury's lines, converted to white by a phosphor coating. Fireworks get their colours from the lines of strontium, barium and copper. Lasers work by driving a single transition over and over. And in this course, these energy levels are the starting point for electron configurations, periodic trends, and the photon energies behind the Beer-Lambert lab.

The energy of hydrogen's lowest level, 2.18 × 10−18 J, is its ionization energy. Multiply by Avogadro's number and you get the 1312 kJ/mol in your textbook's periodic table.

Part 2

The allowed energy levels

Bohr's result for hydrogen is one short equation. Everything else in the lab is built on it.

En = −2.18 × 10−18 Jn2EQN 1

n is the principal quantum number: 1, 2, 3 and so on, with no values in between. n = 1 is the ground state, the lowest energy the electron can have. Every higher n is an excited state.

Why negative? Zero is defined as the energy of an electron that has escaped the atom entirely. A bound electron has less energy than that, so every level is negative. More negative means more tightly held.

Why do the levels crowd together? The gap between levels shrinks as 1/n2. From n = 1 to 2 the energy rises by three quarters of the way to zero. From n = 9 to 10 it barely moves. The diagram is drawn to scale, which is why the top levels pile up. In Part II of the lab you draw this graph by hand.

Part 3

Jumps between levels make the lines

An electron that falls from a higher level to a lower one gives up the difference as one photon. The photon's wavelength is set entirely by that energy gap.

ΔE = Efinal − EinitialEQN 2
ΔE = −2.18 × 10−18 J (1nf2 − 1ni2)EQN 3
λ (m) = hc|ΔE|EQN 4
λ (nm) = λ (m) × 109EQN 5

Watch the sign. When the electron falls (ninitial > nfinal), ΔE comes out negative. That negative sign means the atom lost energy: a photon was emitted. When the electron climbs, ΔE is positive and a photon of exactly that energy must be absorbed. Either way the photon carries |ΔE|, so use the absolute value in Eqn 4.

Series are named by where the electron lands. Every jump that ends on n = 1 belongs to the Lyman series, every jump that ends on n = 2 to the Balmer series, and so on. The landing level sets the size of the gaps, and so the region of the spectrum.

    Part 4

    The whole spectrum, series by series

    Only the Balmer lines fall where eyes can see them. The rest of hydrogen's light is ultraviolet or infrared, and it took better detectors to find it.

    Why the lines bunch up at the left of each row. Jumps from higher and higher levels release almost the same energy, because the upper levels are almost at zero. The lines crowd toward a short-wavelength edge called the series limit. Part 5 lets you push a jump to that edge.

    About the lab's Table 3. The fifteen observed wavelengths you will be asked to assign all come from jumps that start at n ≤ 8. Your Table 2 only goes up to n = 6, which is why four of them will not match anything you calculated. Two are in the Balmer row and two in the Paschen row. The chart shows where to look.

    As ninitial grows, Einitial creeps up to zero, so ΔE approaches the energy of the landing level itself: |ΔE| → 2.18 × 10−18 J ÷ nf2. The wavelength approaches a fixed value, the series limit. For the Balmer series that is 364.5 nm, just past the violet end of what we can see.

    Past the limit is ionization. A photon with more energy than the limit does not lift the electron to another level. It removes the electron from the atom, and any leftover energy becomes the electron's speed. Since that leftover can be anything, the spectrum beyond each limit is continuous, not lines. From the ground state the energy needed is 2.18 × 10−18 J per atom, which is hydrogen's ionization energy.

    Part 6

    How the puzzle was solved

    The lines were measured long before anyone could explain them. The explanation, when it came, rewrote physics.

    1. 1814
      Fraunhofer maps the dark lines in sunlight

      Joseph von Fraunhofer catalogues hundreds of dark lines in the Sun's spectrum. Two of them, which he labels C and F, are hydrogen.

    2. 1862
      Ångström measures hydrogen's visible lines

      Anders Ångström records the four visible lines with enough precision that the pattern can be tested. The unit of wavelength, the ångström, is named for him.

    3. 1868
      Helium is found in the Sun

      A yellow line in the solar spectrum matches no known element. It is named for the Greek sun god. Helium is not isolated on Earth until 1895.

    4. 1885
      Balmer finds the formula

      Johann Balmer, a mathematics teacher in Basel, shows that the four wavelengths fit one formula with a single constant and the integers 3, 4, 5 and 6. He predicts a fifth line, which is found.

    5. 1888
      Rydberg generalizes it

      Johannes Rydberg rewrites Balmer's formula with two integers instead of one. It now predicts whole families of lines in regions no one has measured yet.

    6. 1906–1908
      Lyman and Paschen find the other series

      Theodore Lyman finds the ultraviolet series ending on n = 1. Friedrich Paschen finds the infrared series ending on n = 3. Rydberg's formula was right.

    7. 1913
      Bohr explains it

      Niels Bohr proposes that the electron can only occupy certain orbits, each with a fixed energy, and that light is emitted only when it jumps between them. Eqn 1 falls out of his model, and with it every measured line.

    8. 1922–1924
      Brackett and Pfund series

      Two more infrared series, ending on n = 4 and n = 5, are found exactly where the formula says they should be.

    9. 1925–1926
      Quantum mechanics replaces the orbits

      Heisenberg and Schrödinger replace Bohr's circular orbits with orbitals. For hydrogen, Eqn 1 survives unchanged, which is why you still use it.

    Part 7

    Key terms

    Open each one and say the definition in your own words before you read it.

    Principal quantum number, n

    The whole number that labels an energy level. It can be 1, 2, 3 and so on, never a fraction. Larger n means higher energy and an electron found farther from the nucleus on average.

    Ground state

    The lowest energy level, n = 1. An undisturbed hydrogen atom sits here. Its energy is −2.18 × 10−18 J.

    Excited state

    Any level above the ground state. An atom gets there by absorbing energy from heat, an electric discharge or a photon. It does not stay long.

    Photon

    A single packet of light. Its energy is E = hc/λ. Shorter wavelength means higher energy: ultraviolet photons carry more energy than visible ones, which carry more than infrared.

    Emission

    Light given off when an electron falls to a lower level. The photon's energy equals the size of the drop. Emission spectra are bright lines on a dark background.

    Absorption

    Light taken in when an electron climbs to a higher level. Only photons whose energy exactly matches a gap are absorbed. Absorption spectra are dark lines on a bright background, like Fraunhofer's.

    Spectral series

    All the lines from jumps that end on the same level. Lyman ends on n = 1, Balmer on 2, Paschen on 3, Brackett on 4, Pfund on 5.

    Series limit

    The shortest wavelength a series can reach, from a jump that starts infinitely far up. For Balmer it is 364.5 nm. Beyond it the spectrum is continuous.

    Ionization energy

    The energy needed to remove the electron from the atom completely. For hydrogen in its ground state it is 2.18 × 10−18 J per atom, or 1312 kJ per mole.

    Planck's constant, h

    6.626 × 10−34 J·s. It links a photon's energy to its frequency and, with the speed of light, to its wavelength.

    Wavelength, λ

    The distance between successive crests of a light wave. Visible light runs from about 400 nm (violet) to 700 nm (red). The lab uses 350 to 700 nm as its working range for the visible region.

    Quantized

    Restricted to certain separate values with nothing in between. The energy of an electron in an atom is quantized, which is the whole reason the spectrum is lines instead of a smear.

    Mistakes that cost points every year

    • Forgetting to square n. E4 uses 16, not 4. If your n = 10 energy is −0.218 × 10−18 J you divided by 10.
    • Mixing up which n is which. In Eqn 3, nf is where the electron lands. For emission it is the smaller number. If your ΔE comes out positive for a drop, the two n values are swapped.
    • Feeding a negative ΔE into Eqn 4. A wavelength cannot be negative. Use |ΔE|.
    • Stopping at metres. 6.56 × 10−7 m is correct but Table 2 wants 656 nm. Multiply by 109.
    • Rounding too early. Keep three significant figures through the calculation. Your calculated lines will sit within about half a nanometre of the observed ones; a wrong jump is off by tens of nanometres, so matching is unambiguous if you have not rounded.
    • Wrong graph scale. The y-axis on the lab's graph paper runs from 0 to −2.0 × 10−18 J. Plot n = 1 at the very bottom and n = 10 almost on the zero line.

    Quick sanity checks

    • Jumps ending on n = 2 should land between 365 and 656 nm. Anything else is in the wrong series.
    • The 3 → 2 jump is the longest Balmer wavelength and the smallest Balmer energy. Larger drops give shorter wavelengths.
    • Every Lyman line is below 122 nm. Every Paschen line is above 820 nm.
